Snails vs Knights

An analysis of medieval manuscripts:

Introduction

Imagine life as a noble in medieval times. You are reading your family psalter, a prayer book of Psalms, when you notice something strange in the margin: a fully armored knight fighting a snail! This was a lot more common than one may think. Curators at the British Library note that images of armed knights fighting snails are common in 13th and 14th century manuscripts, especially in the margins (Ancient, Medieval, and Early Modern Manuscripts [AMEMM], 2013). Scholars have discovered multiple manuscripts from many regions of Western Europe, dating from the 13th to the 15th centuries, that depict snails in combat with knights (Santos, 2021, p. 217). These images typically appear in religious texts and almost always sit in the margins. Many of the examples in this exhibit come from psalters or books of hours, but they also appear in decretals, books of church law, such as the image below. Historians still debate what the snails truly mean, but this exhibit argues that they were most likely meant to be funny, one joke among many in a margin full of absurd scenes. It presents a sample of manuscripts that show snails fighting knights, along with an analysis of their potential meanings.

Marginalia

Illuminated manuscripts, handmade books decorated with gold, rich colors, and detailed artwork, were a major part of the medieval period and first appeared around the 5th century CE and remained prominent through the 1500’s. They gave the wealthy a luxurious way to read important religious texts, and the high quality materials used helped preserve a lot of medieval literature. Kings and queens owned psalters studded with gemstones, coated in gold, and colored with vivid and rare pigments. Making them was a complicated and expensive process, but those who could afford them cherished them and passed them down. Cheaper manuscripts were available, with less color, smaller print, and simpler designs, for those who couldn’t afford a fully illuminated copy. Though these books were meant to be read, and contained religious songs and stories, many also included detailed pictures, with some form of artwork in the margins of nearly every page. The marginalia could relate to what was written, and Nelles (2003, p. 68) notes they “frequently gloss or parody the accompanying text,” though many have no clear link to it (p. 72).

Around the 13th century, drôlerie (playful, often absurd figures drawn in the margins) became a popular way to fill the margins of manuscripts. This coincided with the decline of scriptoria in religious houses at the end of the 13th century, when the rise of the university system led professional scribes in towns to take over the monks’ job (Kauffmann, 2015). Drôlerie stayed popular until about the late 15th century before it gave way to more naturalistic designs. Among the many possibilities in the margins of those earlier pages, one theme kept returning, especially from the 1290s through the mid-14th century: the knight fighting the snail (Lyons, 2025).

A Comedic Battle

So why have we found so many depictions of snails and knights in combat? It isn’t reality, so what purpose does it serve? The first reason comes from the type of marginalia discussed earlier: drôlerie. It is amusing, or at least bizarre, to see a snail not only fighting knights but often defeating them (AMEMM, 2013). One depiction even shows a knight kneeling before a snail with a worried look on his face. While this is a plausible reason, there is another possibility not far off from the humor theory.

Historian Lilian M. C. Randall argued that the snails represented the Lombards, a way of calling them “avaricious, malicious, and cowardly” (Randall, 1962, p. 362). She pointed to references in France where Lombards were thought to be unhygienic, and many were also usurers and pawnbrokers (Nelles, 2003, p. 77). This is definitely a possibility, but it has limits, since it doesn’t explain why the knight is so often on the losing end (AMEMM, 2013). In a broader context, the snails fit into a world of chaos and randomness.

An Upside-down World

When we widen the scope of research, many other bizarre images can be found in manuscripts. Alongside the violent snails, animals play instruments, cats and mice feud with the mice as victors, and a rabbit even hunts a dog as seen in the British Library images below. This raises the question: did the snails carry a specific symbolism or metaphor, or do they belong to a broader context of wacky creatures? Medieval artists are often teased for their awkward drawings of animals, since many didn’t get to see exotic creatures they drew first hand, but the scenes shown here are different. They are otherworldly. Lyons (2025) describes scenes like these as comic inversions that puncture seriousness, safe because they sit at the edge of the page. Nelles (2003, p. 76) calls the knight cowering before a snail an example of the monde renversé, “in which the weak intimidate the strong,” a world where mice defeat cats, rabbits hunt dogs, animals make music, and snails fight knights.

Conclusion

Although there could be a connection between snails and Lombardy, the humorous nature of drôlerie and the many other examples of creatures doing strange things suggest that the most likely purpose of the snails is humor and the irony of an alternate reality. No matter the text they appear in, and no matter the reader’s background, these wacky drawings were likely good for a laugh.
